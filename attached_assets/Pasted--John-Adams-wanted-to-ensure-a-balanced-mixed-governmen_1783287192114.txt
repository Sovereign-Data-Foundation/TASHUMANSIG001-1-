**John Adams wanted to ensure a balanced, mixed government with strong separation of powers so that no single interest—especially wealth, commerce, or emerging concentrations of economic power—could dominate the republic or place itself above the law.**

### Adams’ Core Concerns

Adams was deeply wary of **unchecked aristocracy**, including what we would recognize as economic or “artificial” aristocracy based on wealth, birth, and commercial influence. He distinguished this from a “natural aristocracy” of virtue and talent. In his writings (notably *Defence of the Constitutions of Government of the United States* and letters to Thomas Jefferson), he argued:

- **Inequality of wealth** is inevitable in a free society and creates natural influence (“the five pillars of aristocracy”: beauty, wealth, birth, genius, and virtue). Any one of the first three could overpower the latter two if left unchecked.
- Without proper constitutional **checks and balances**, the rich and well-connected would form factions or cabals that corrupt legislatures, tilt laws in their favor, and undermine popular sovereignty.
- **No group is above the law**: Corporate or commercial interests (merchants, banks, chartered companies, etc.) must remain subordinate to the sovereign people and the “natural law of the land” (reason, justice, and the common good). He saw the British East India Company and similar monopolies as cautionary examples of concentrated power threatening liberty.

Adams advocated for:

- A strong **executive** to check legislative excess.
- An independent **judiciary**.
- A **bicameral legislature** (with a senate as a stabilizing force against pure majoritarianism or mob influence).
- A system where virtue and public service, not mere wealth, guided leadership.

He famously warned that **“a Constitution of Government once changed from Freedom, can never be restored”** and emphasized that republics require **virtue** in the people and leaders. Commerce and industry were positive, but the “spirit of commerce” unchecked by morality could erode the republic.

### Tie to Your Point

Your statement — **“No corporate interest is immune to the natural law of the land”** — aligns directly with Adams’ view. He did not want corporations (or any economic faction) to enjoy sovereign-like immunity or override the collective will of the people. Government exists to protect individual rights and the public good, not to serve as a tool for private economic power.

This connects to the broader conversation on **popular sovereignty** and **dual sovereignty**: The people (at federal and state levels) remain supreme. Any institutional or corporate power derives from laws passed under that sovereignty and remains accountable to it.

Adams’ solution was structural — design government to channel human nature (ambition, self-interest, inequality) into productive balance rather than domination.
