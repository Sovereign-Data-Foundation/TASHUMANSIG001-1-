The Foundations of Adamsian Republicanism: Dual Sovereignty and Double Security
The foundational architecture of early American constitutionalism rests upon the axiom that stable liberty requires a "government of laws, and not of men". In synthesizing historical precedent with Enlightenment political philosophy, John Adams formulated a highly realistic, structurally checked model of republican governance designed to withstand the corrupting influence of unchecked wealth and power. At the core of this system are the dual principles of dual sovereignty and double security, which operate as mutual checks within a highly integrated federal structure. [1][2][3][4]
Dual sovereignty establishes that power does not flow top-down from a centralized national government, nor does it reside solely within state-level assemblies; rather, power originates directly from the sovereign people, who divide and delegate their authority across state and national levels. Under this federal blueprint, the states retain their reserved powers to manage local governance, civic infrastructure, and internal commerce, while the national government exercises explicitly delegated powers over foreign affairs, national defense, and interstate trade.
This geographical division of power is reinforced by the mechanism of double security. By establishing overlapping jurisdictions and a tripartite division of powers at both the state and national levels, the constitutional structure prevents any single economic class or faction from capturing the entire administrative apparatus. If a monied aristocracy or an aggressive populist faction succeeds in dominating a local state assembly, its influence is mitigated and countered by the national government, and vice versa. Power is set against power, and interest against interest, ensuring that the supremacy of law remains inviolable and that no private entity can achieve sovereign immunity or stand above the public interest. [1][2][3][4]
The Containment of the Natural Aristocracy: The Separate Chamber Mechanism
The primary threat to any republic, in Adams' view, was the inevitable emergence of a "natural aristocracy". Unlike his contemporary Thomas Jefferson, who maintained a more optimistic view of human nature and believed that education could foster a "natural aristocracy" of virtue and talent alone, Adams took a highly skeptical view of social stratification. He argued that in any civilized society, inequalities of wealth, birth, beauty, and intellect are inescapable, and that those possessed of "splendid wealth" inevitably acquire an outsized degree of social admiration, influence, and political power. [1][2][3][4]
Adams directed his sharpest critiques against the French economic theorist Anne-Robert-Jacques Turgot, who advocated for a government concentrated in a single, unicameral assembly. Turgot argued that because America had abolished hereditary titles, its citizens were equal, making a bicameral legislature with separate "orders" or a senate unnecessary and divisive. Adams countered that a single legislative chamber was the most dangerous system imaginable, warning that "a representative assembly... if you give them all the power, legislative, executive, and judicial... would invade the liberties of the people... sooner and oftener than an absolute monarchy". [1][2][3][4]
Within a unicameral assembly, the wealthy elite would inevitably use their resources, patronage, and influence to corrupt the legislative process. Because they are mixed with the general representatives of the people, they can easily divide the assembly or secure a majority, dominating the state with the tragic "consent and acclamations of the people themselves". Adams' structural solution was not the futile attempt to eradicate wealth or outlaw inequality, but to isolate and neutralize it within a separate legislative chamber—the Senate. [1][2][3][4]

The Subordination of Factions and the Early Republic’s Anti-Monopoly Doctrine
While Adams designed the bicameral legislature to contain the natural aristocracy of personal fortunes, the founding generation was equally preoccupied with preventing the rise of artificial, legally constructed monopolies. The American colonists’ hostility toward concentrated corporate power was forged during the revolutionary struggle against the British East India Company, whose state-sanctioned monopoly over trade was viewed as an existential threat to local liberties, culminating in the symbolic protest of the Boston Tea Party. [1][2]
This deep-seated aversion to corporate privilege led to intense debates during the drafting and ratification of the federal Constitution. Anti-Federalists, along with prominent figures such as George Mason and Thomas Jefferson, expressed grave concerns regarding the potential for the new federal government to issue exclusive corporate charters and grant monopolies. Mason and Jefferson argued forcefully for the inclusion of an explicit anti-monopoly clause in the Constitution. Ultimately, the Framers at Philadelphia deliberately chose not to grant Congress the general power to charter corporations, leaving the federal government with only the narrowly enumerated authority to grant exclusive rights under patent and copyright laws to promote science and the useful arts. [1][2]
In the early decades of the Republic, business corporations were strictly subordinated to the sovereignty of the state legislatures. Under the classical regulatory regime, a corporation was not a right-bearing entity, but an artificial instrument of state policy chartered on a case-by-case basis to perform specific public-benefit functions, such as building toll bridges, municipal water systems, or canals. This sentiment was famously echoed by Thomas Jefferson in 1816: "I hope we shall crush in its birth the aristocracy of our monied corporations, which dare already to challenge our government to a trial of strength and bid defiance to the laws of their country". [1][2][3][4]
The Judicial Metamorphosis: From Public Utility to Corporate Personhood
Throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the original republican model of corporate subordination was systematically dismantled by a series of landmark judicial decisions. Rather than maintaining their status as regulated instruments of public utility, corporations successfully leveraged the courts to acquire the constitutional rights and protections originally intended for natural human citizens. [1][2][3][4]
The first major shift occurred in Trustees of Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819), where the Supreme Court had to determine whether the state of New Hampshire could modify a corporate charter originally granted to Dartmouth College by King George III. Chief Justice John Marshall, while famously defining a corporation as an "artificial being, invisible, intangible, and existing only in contemplation of law," ultimately ruled that a corporate charter is a private contract protected under Article I, Section 10 of the Constitution, which prohibits states from passing laws "impairing the obligation of contracts". This decision insulated corporations from retrospective legislative modification, providing the legal foundation for the modern, autonomous business enterprise. [1][2][3][4]
The next critical evolutionary leap took place in Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad Company (1886). In a tax dispute regarding the assessment of railroad properties, Chief Justice Morrison Waite declared before oral arguments that the Court would not hear arguments on whether the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause applied to corporations, stating: "We are all of opinion that it does". By extending the protections of the Fourteenth Amendment—originally enacted to safeguard the civil rights of newly freed African Americans—to corporate entities, the Court effectively granted corporations "personhood" status. Reclaiming State Sovereignty: The Corporate Power Reset
To counter this judicial imbalance, modern constitutional scholars have developed a state-level strategy known as the "Corporate Power Reset". This legal theory asserts that while Citizens United prevents governments from regulating a corporation's rights once it exists, the Supreme Court does not possess the authority to define what a corporation actually is or what powers a state may choose to grant it. Because corporations are pure creatures of state statute, the ultimate authority to define, limit, or withdraw their corporate powers remains vested exclusively in the individual states. [1][2][3][4][5][6][7]
By amending state general incorporation acts to explicitly deny corporate entities the statutory power to engage in political spending, states can bypass the free-speech hurdles established by Citizens United. As Justice Antonin Scalia noted in his concurring opinion in Citizens United, "To be sure in 1791 (as now) corporations could pursue only the objectives set forth in their charters". If a state-level charter restricts a corporate entity's functional capacities from the moment of its creation, the entity lacks the legal capacity to act outside those boundaries, rendering its constitutional free-speech rights practically moot. This strategy seeks to re-establish the subordinate status of commercial entities, ensuring that no private interest can stand above the public welfare. [1][2][3][4][5][6][7]
